Three Demons and a Baby

A tale from Blackthorn Gate

The baby arrived at Blackthorn Gate in a peach basket, wrapped in a blue blanket and wearing one woolen sock.

This was unusual even for Blackthorn Gate, where the road crossed from the green country into places maps preferred to decorate with warnings. Travelers left offerings there: coins, apples, once a very apologetic goat. They did not generally leave infants.

Grimble found her at dawn. He was the largest of the three gatekeepers, with horns like old roots and a voice that loosened dust from ceilings.

“It has no papers,” he observed.

“Perhaps the papers are under it,” offered Nix, who had six elegant fingers on each hand and a professional interest in loopholes.

The third demon, Auntie Brim, lifted the baby. Brim was nobody’s aunt, but she had acquired the title after six hundred years of telling everyone to wear a cloak. The basket held no papers. Beneath the blanket lay a silver button, a crust of bread, and a sprig of rosemary tied with red thread.

The baby opened her eyes.

“Oh,” murmured Brim.

It was not the sort of oh one uses upon discovering treasure. It was the quieter kind, used when treasure discovers you.

They brought the basket into the gatehouse. Blackthorn Gate had iron doors, arrow slits, and a kitchen designed by someone who believed soup was a military resource. They set the baby on the long table between a bowl of turnips and the Book of Those Who May Not Pass.

“What do human babies eat?” Grimble wondered.

“Small portions, presumably.” Nix consulted the index. The Book contained regulations for kings in disguise, witches in pursuit, talking wolves, silent wolves, and geese of uncertain allegiance. It contained nothing about babies.

The baby began to cry.

The sound filled the gatehouse with astonishing efficiency. Grimble checked the alarm bells. Nix attempted three minor illusions: a butterfly, a moon, and a dancing sausage. The baby rejected each with increasing conviction.

Brim dipped a finger into the soup and tasted it.

“Too much pepper.”

She found goat’s milk, warmed it, and fed the baby with a clean corner of cloth. Silence settled by degrees. Outside, mist combed the dark pines. Somewhere beyond them, something enormous sneezed and frightened a flock of crows into the sky.

The baby held Brim’s finger. Around her wrist was a pale mark shaped like a little crown, though none of the demons noticed because Grimble had just remembered that babies leaked.

By noon, the gatehouse had changed allegiance.

Grimble surrendered his helmet for a cradle. Nix cut strips from the velvet curtain for nappies, insisting the curtain had always been unnecessarily ceremonial. Brim discovered that the ancient battle hymn of the Ashen Host made an excellent lullaby if one omitted the verses about rivers of flame.

They named the child Pip, because she was small and because the peach basket had put the idea into their heads.

❦ ❦ ❦

For seven days, nobody came looking for her.

On the eighth, a royal carriage appeared on the green-country road. It came without trumpets. Mud covered its painted crest, but not completely; beneath one wheel, a golden wren still showed its wing.

Three riders accompanied it. Their armor was polished, their smiles more so.

“We seek a stolen child,” announced the captain. “By order of the Regent, all baskets, bundles, and suspiciously shaped parcels are to be searched.”

Nix leaned against the gate. “We have two baskets, eleven bundles, and a parcel shaped exactly like a regrettable hat. You must be specific.”

From inside came a hiccup.

The captain’s smile remained, although the warmth departed from it. “A baby girl. Blue blanket. Silver royal clasp.”

Brim glanced at the button on the mantel. Tarnish had hidden most of its design, but morning firelight now caught a tiny golden wren.

Grimble’s tail stopped thumping Pip’s cradle.

“We found no clasp,” Brim replied.

This was true in the narrow way truths sometimes become useful.

The riders dismounted.

Blackthorn Gate had stood for nine hundred years because everyone understood its single rule: those admitted by the gatekeepers could pass, and those refused could try the swamp. The swamp had rules too, but it kept them private.

The captain rested a hand on his sword. “The Regent will reward cooperation.”

Pip hiccupped again.

Nix smiled with all his teeth. “We already have a goat.”

The fight, when it came, was brief. Grimble lifted the carriage and turned it around. Nix persuaded the horses, through means both magical and complimentary, to gallop home. Brim did nothing visible at all. The captain discovered this was worst of the three options when his sword became too heavy to lift and his boots began walking toward the swamp.

“Tell your Regent,” Brim called after him, “that Blackthorn Gate is closed.”

“To everyone?” asked Grimble.

Brim watched the muddy carriage vanish into the mist. Pip had begun chewing thoughtfully on the edge of the Book.

“To paperwork.”

❦ ❦ ❦

That night, while rain tapped the roof with a thousand small fingers, the demons examined the basket again. Nix teased apart the red thread around the rosemary. Hidden inside its knot was a strip of paper no wider than a match.

Keep her from the crown until the crown is hers.

No signature followed. Only the faint scent of peaches.

Grimble stared at Pip, asleep in his helmet. “That seems important.”

“It can wait until morning,” Brim murmured, drawing the blue blanket higher.

Years passed at Blackthorn Gate, as years do everywhere, though perhaps with more smoke.

Pip learned to walk along the parapet, holding Grimble’s smallest finger. Nix taught her letters, card tricks, and the legal distinction between trespassing and arriving unexpectedly. Brim taught her which mushrooms cured fever, which mushrooms caused visions, and which mushrooms merely tasted disappointing.

Travelers brought rumors. The Regent taxed windows, then shadows, then complaints about taxes. In the capital, the palace gates were chained. The golden wren vanished from flags.

Pip grew. The mark on her wrist sharpened into a crown.

On her sixteenth birthday, she found the silver button in Brim’s sewing box.

Nobody spoke for a while.

Outside, spring had reached the border. White flowers crowded the ditch, and the old blackthorns wore green at their tips. The road into the human country curved away through sunlight.

“You knew,” Pip breathed.

“Not at first,” Grimble answered.

“Not enough,” Nix added.

Brim placed the hidden message beside the button. Her hands, which had once held fortress walls against armies, trembled only slightly.

Pip read it twice. Then she looked at the three creatures who had lied to soldiers, ruined a curtain, and spent sixteen years pretending not to worry whenever she climbed anything taller than a chair.

“I suppose I have a crown to collect.”

Grimble reached for his helmet, remembered its history, and chose another. Nix packed maps, money, and the regrettable hat. Brim wrapped bread and rosemary in a blue cloth.

At noon, Blackthorn Gate opened.

Four figures crossed into the green country: a girl with one silver button at her throat, and three demons carrying far too much luggage. Behind them, the gatehouse windows caught the sun. Ahead, the road shone after rain, bright in places and dark in others, as roads tend to be when they are going somewhere important.

In the ditch, almost hidden among the white flowers, grew a young peach tree no one remembered planting.

Home is who walks
beside you.

A lilting song for iron helmets, open roads, and the sort of family that turns royal carriages around.

Verse 1

At Blackthorn Gate where the black boughs lean,
Where the road slips out of the country green,
Three demons woke to the morning rain
And a peachwood basket beside the lane.
A blue-wrapped baby, one woolen sock,
A sprig of rosemary tied in a knot—
Grimble frowned and Nix checked twice,
While Brim warmed milk by the firelight.

Chorus

Oh, bar the door and mend the flame,
The smallest hearts need room to grow.
A crown may give a child a name,
But home is made by those who know
When to stand and when to stay,
When to let the whole world wait.
Three demons and a child named Pip,
Safe behind Blackthorn Gate.

Verse 2

Grimble gave her his iron helm,
The finest cradle in all the realm.
Nix cut nappies from velvet red
And blamed the curtains overhead.
Brim sang low of an ashen host,
But left out rivers and burning coasts.
Pip held one finger, fierce and small,
And conquered the keep without trying at all.

Verse 3

Then down the road came riders three,
With polished smiles and a royal decree:
“A stolen child in a blanket blue,
A silver clasp—we know it’s true.”
Brim saw the wren in the firelight gleam,
Grimble turned their carriage team,
And Nix sent horses galloping straight—
For paperwork stops at Blackthorn Gate.

Verse 4

Deep in the rosemary’s scarlet thread,
A little line lay quietly hid:
Keep her far from the crown’s command,
Till the crown is hers by rightful hand.
They folded the warning and dimmed the light;
Some truths can wait for morning bright.
Outside, in rain no eye could see,
A peach stone dreamed of becoming a tree.

Bridge

Sixteen springs on the old stone wall,
Sixteen winters in the western hall,
Letters and tricks and mushroom lore,
Small muddy boots by the kitchen door.
Then Pip found the button, bright and round,
And read the truth without a sound.
Three mighty hands began to shake—
For love must open what fear would lock.

Final Verse

At noon the thorns wore tips of green,
White flowers stirred where frost had been.
Pip pinned the silver beneath her chin;
The gate swung wide to let them begin.
Brim packed bread, and Nix packed gold,
Grimble chose a spare helm, old.
Four went out where the wet road shone,
Three demons, one queen, and all of them home.

Final Chorus

Oh, leave the door and guard the flame,
The bravest hearts must learn to go.
A crown may give the world a name,
But home is who walks beside you slow.
Through the bright and through the gray,
Past the fear that barred the way,
Three demons and a girl named Pip
Walked beyond Blackthorn Gate.

And low among the flowers white,
A peach tree lifted into light.

Beyond the gate

Some doors keep danger out. The best ones know when to open.

The road ahead is bright in places and dark in others, as roads tend to be when they are going somewhere important.
